## The central legal question, in plain language

The Supreme Court of Canada decided in *Pecore v. Pecore* (2007) and its companion case *Madsen Estate v. Saylor* (2007) that when a parent puts their adult child onto a bank or investment account as a joint owner — without the child contributing money to the account — there is a **legal presumption that the parent did not mean to give the account to the child**. Instead, the law presumes the child is holding the account in trust for the parent (and after the parent's death, in trust for the parent's estate).

The presumption can be overturned, but only if the child can prove — with actual evidence — that the parent intended to make a real gift of the right of survivorship.

What kind of evidence? The Supreme Court was specific:

- **Bank or brokerage forms** that explicitly say the parent meant the child to keep the balance after death (a generic "right of survivorship" checkbox is *not* enough — *Madsen* established that).
- **Written notes, letters, or advisor records** showing what the parent intended at the time the account was opened or modified.
- **How the account was actually used** during the parent's lifetime (was the child treating the money as theirs, or was it strictly the parent's money with the child as a convenience signatory?).
- **What the will says** about how the estate is supposed to be divided.

## Daughter's six numbered questions, answered plainly

### Q1 — What is the significance of April 21, 2026 in terms of asset ownership?

That date is significant for three different reasons, in three different legal systems:

1. **Income-tax law (federal):** It is the moment of *deemed disposition*. Mary is treated, for tax purposes, as having sold all her capital property at its fair market value immediately before her death. The accrued capital gains on those assets become taxable on Mary's final (terminal) 2026 income tax return. The RIF is similarly deemed to have been received in full immediately before death.
2. **Property law (provincial):** Where assets were legally held jointly with right of survivorship, legal title passes to the surviving joint holder (Daughter) on that date. But *legal title* and *beneficial ownership* are not the same — see the *Pecore* analysis above.
3. **Wills and estates law (provincial):** Mary's will takes effect on that date. Her intent, as expressed in the will, is "frozen" for legal interpretation as of 2026-04-21.

(Detail: `findings/deemed-disposition.md` Entry 1; `findings/jtwros-treatment.md` Entry 1.)

### Q2 — When can Daughter disperse funds to beneficiaries?

The honest answer: **the earliest fully-safe distribution is after CRA issues a clearance certificate, which is realistically late 2027 to mid-2028**.

The sequence:

1. File Mary's terminal T1 return (due **2027-04-30**).
2. Get the Notice of Assessment back from CRA. Pay any tax owing.
3. File any required estate trust return (T3).
4. Once those are settled, file Form TX19 with CRA asking for a clearance certificate.
5. CRA reviews and issues the clearance (currently runs ~4 months under their service standards, though variable).
6. Once the clearance certificate is in hand, distribution can happen without Daughter being personally on the hook for any later-discovered tax.

**Interim partial distributions are possible** earlier than that, provided that (a) all known and reasonably-expected tax has been provisioned for with adequate holdback, and (b) the *Pecore* allocation has been resolved with the lawyer. So a partial distribution to Brother could conceivably happen in late 2026 or 2027, but only after the Pecore question is settled and the tax accountant signs off on the holdback.

There is also a common-law "executor's year" benchmark — estates are conventionally wound up within 12 months of death — but it is a soft guideline, not a hard deadline. It does not override the CRA clearance-certificate rule.

### Q3 — When will taxes be due?

Multiple deadlines, in order:

| Deadline | What is due |
|---|---|
| When Daughter applies for probate (if probate is required) | Ontario Estate Administration Tax — paid as a deposit with the court application |
| **2027-04-30** | Mary's **2026 terminal T1** (income tax for the year of death) and any tax balance owing — April 30 of the year following death under *Income Tax Act* s. 150(1)(d). Note: the "6 months after death" rule that some practitioners cite (s. 150(1)(b)) *does not* apply to the terminal return for someone who died before November. |
| 180 days after the Certificate of Appointment is issued (if probate) | Estate Information Return to Ontario Ministry of Finance |
| 90 days after the estate's chosen first year-end | First T3 estate trust return (CRA) |
| Annually thereafter | Further T3 returns until the estate is wound up |

Mary's 2025 T1 (the regular return for the year *before* death) was filed before her death — so there is no prior-year filing deadline.

### Q4 — What happens when the bank decides on probate (yes and no)?

**If the bank decides no probate is needed:**

- The bank releases Mary's chequing/savings balances to Daughter, treating them as having passed by survivorship.
- No probate application is filed. No Ontario Estate Administration Tax is owed. No Estate Information Return is required.
- *But:* the bank's decision is administrative, not judicial. It does **not** resolve the *Pecore* question between Daughter and Brother. The bank releases the funds based on legal title; the law of beneficial ownership operates separately.

**If the bank decides probate is required:**

- Daughter applies to the Ontario Superior Court of Justice for a *Certificate of Appointment of Estate Trustee* (or a *Small Estate Certificate* if the value is ≤ $150,000).
- The probate application identifies the estate's assets. Brother must be served with a copy before the application is filed. This is the moment when the *Pecore* allocation — which assets Daughter is treating as part of the estate vs. as her own — becomes explicit and visible to Brother.
- Estate Administration Tax is owed at $15 per $1,000 (or part thereof) of the value of the estate over $50,000. So an estate worth $250,000 in the EAT base would owe $15 × 200 = $3,000.
- Within 180 days of the Certificate being issued, an Estate Information Return must be filed with the Ontario Ministry of Finance.

(Detail: `findings/probate-and-eat.md`.)

### Q5 — What exactly in the estate is subject to taxation? Is it the full asset value, or some portion?

It depends which tax. There are two completely different tax systems at work:

**Ontario Estate Administration Tax (provincial, on probate):**
- Applies to the *full value* of assets in the EAT base, less encumbrances on real property only. There is no cost-base subtraction.
- The first $50,000 of estate value is exempt; the rate above that is $15 per $1,000.
- Assets that pass by survivorship or by named beneficiary (like the RIF) are *generally* not included in the EAT base — *but* if a *Pecore* analysis finds that Mary was the beneficial owner of the joint assets, those assets fall under the EAT line "all property of the deceased that was held in another person's name" and are included.

**Income tax (federal + provincial, on Mary's terminal T1):**
- Applies to the *capital gain* on capital property — the difference between fair market value at death and Mary's original cost. Not the full value. Currently 50% of the gain is included in taxable income (verify the 2026 inclusion rate — there has been recent legislative activity at this level).
- Applies to the *full FMV* of the RIF, included in income on Mary's terminal return.
- Cash and bank balances generally have no capital gain — just any accrued interest to the date of death.

So the same $1 of asset can be hit by EAT on its full value (if probated) *and* hit by income tax on the embedded capital gain. They are separate taxes; one does not displace the other.
