## Q&A captured 2026-05-14 (joint-ownership fact pattern)

The following facts came out of conversation on 2026-05-14 and bear directly on whether the joint accounts and non-registered investments truly belong to Daughter by right of survivorship, or whether they fall back into Mary's estate to be split with Brother.

- **Origin of joint titling:** The joint bank accounts and non-registered investments were originally Mary's. Daughter was added to the titles later. (This is the classic *Pecore v. Pecore* 2007 SCC 17 fact pattern.)
- **Documented intent:** Daughter has not yet checked whether the bank/brokerage account-opening forms, advisor notes, or any letters/memos document Mary's intent regarding survivorship. Currently no known documented gift-of-survivorship intent. **Action item:** check account-opening documents and ask the financial advisor whether contemporaneous notes exist.
- **Lifetime use:** The joint accounts were operated solely for Mary's benefit during her lifetime — her income, her bills, her expenses. Daughter did not use the funds for her own purposes. This is consistent with a "convenience" / resulting-trust pattern rather than true beneficial joint ownership.
- **Brother's awareness:** Brother knows the accounts and investments were jointly held between Mary and Daughter. He has not raised any objection. However, the *financial consequence* (that Daughter would take all joint assets by survivorship, leaving only minor assets in the residue to be split) has not been explicitly discussed. Neither Brother nor Daughter currently understands the implication.
- **Testamentary intent:** Both Brother and Daughter have read the will. Both understand Mary's expressed intent to be a substantially equal split of her estate between the two of them (apart from specified jewellery).

### Implication (researcher's note, not legal advice)

Under *Pecore v. Pecore* (Supreme Court of Canada, 2007), when a parent gratuitously adds an adult child to a bank account or investment, there is a **rebuttable presumption of resulting trust** — meaning the asset is presumed to belong to the parent's estate, not to pass to the adult child by survivorship, unless evidence of a contrary intention is shown.

On the four facts above (parent-funded, no documented contrary intent, convenience-only lifetime use, will directs equal split), the presumption of resulting trust looks unlikely to be rebutted. A court could find the joint assets are held in resulting trust for the estate and form part of the residue split 50/50 with Brother. The will itself — directing an equal split — is evidence courts use to assess Mary's intent for the joint accounts.

### Two practical flags arising from this

1. **The retitling papers Daughter signed with the financial advisor** (to move investments from jointly held to Daughter's sole name) may be premature. Completing the retitling before the *Pecore* question is resolved could be construed as Daughter, as executor, taking estate assets for herself in breach of fiduciary duty to Brother. **Action item:** consider asking the financial advisor to pause the retitling pending advice from an Ontario estates lawyer.
2. **This is almost certainly the source of the financial-advisor vs tax-accountant disagreement** referenced above. The advisor is treating the joint accounts under pre-*Pecore* survivorship logic; the accountant is likely aware of *Pecore* and is taking a more conservative position.

## Daughter's settled distribution intent

Independent of the *Pecore* legal question, **Daughter intends to split the estate's remaining assets 50/50 with Brother** after all estate expenses are paid — exactly as the will directs. This is a settled position, not contingent on legal compulsion. It means the *Pecore* analysis is not about "who gets the money" (both siblings get approximately the same outcome economically either way). It is about **which legal and tax mechanism** is used to reach that outcome — primarily a question of how filings are made, whether EAT is owed, and how the distribution is documented and defended.

Two mechanisms reach the same end state:

- **Path A** (financial advisor's current plan): Daughter completes the retitling, takes the joint assets in her sole name by survivorship, then gives Brother approximately half as a personal gift. No gift tax in Canada, but the tax treatment and documentation sit with Daughter personally and create an awkward inconsistency between the legal records ("Daughter owns it") and the actual disposition ("split with Brother").
- **Path B** (likely the *Pecore*-consistent characterization on Mary's facts): Joint assets are recognized as part of Mary's beneficially-owned estate, pass through probate if BMO requires it, and the estate distributes per the will. EAT of approximately $6,450 may apply, but the filings, the estate accounting, and the distribution are coherent and align with what the will says.

The lawyer's role is to advise on the choice of mechanism, the supporting documentation, and how to explain the choice clearly to Brother. Both siblings are aligned on the outcome — the question is just how to get there cleanly.
