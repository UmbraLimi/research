# JTWROS Treatment of Mary's Joint Accounts and Investments

**Topic:** Whether the joint bank accounts and non-registered investments held in joint names of Mary and Daughter pass to Daughter by right of survivorship (and beneficially as Daughter's), or whether they are held in resulting trust for Mary's estate (and beneficially Mary's at the moment of death).

**Why this matters for Daughter (Q1, Q2, Q4, Q5, Q6 of `Background.md`):** Given Daughter's settled intent to split the estate's residue 50/50 with Brother per the will (`Background.md`, Daughter's distribution intent paragraph), the *Pecore* question is not about *who gets the money* — both siblings end up in approximately the same place economically either way. It is about which legal characterization fits Mary's facts, which then drives: (i) the mechanism of distribution (Path A through Daughter as gift, or Path B through the estate per the will), (ii) Ontario EAT exposure (≈$0 vs ≈$6,450 if probate is required), (iii) how Mary's terminal-T1 capital gains are reported, and (iv) the defensibility of the chosen path against later CRA review.

## Entry 1 — Pecore v. Pecore, 2007 SCC 17

**Captured:** 2026-05-14
**Source:** Supreme Court of Canada, decided 2007-05-03 (heard 2006-12-06).
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/2355/index.do (case page; full judgment body served via iframe at `/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/2355/index.do?iframe=true`)
**Citation:** *Pecore v. Pecore*, 2007 SCC 17, [2007] 1 SCR 795.
**Panel:** McLachlin C.J. and Bastarache, Binnie, LeBel, Deschamps, Fish, Abella, Charron and Rothstein JJ. (9 judges).
**Decision:** Rothstein J. writing for the majority of 8; Abella J. concurring in the result on different reasoning. Appeal dismissed.
**Source quality:** Primary. Binding Supreme Court of Canada authority. Still good law in 2026.

### Why this case controls Mary's situation

The fact pattern in *Pecore* is almost exactly Mary's: an ageing parent gratuitously placed the bulk of their assets into joint accounts and joint investment holdings with one of their adult children. The Supreme Court used *Pecore* (and its companion case *Madsen Estate v. Saylor*, 2007 SCC 18) to set out the rule that applies across Canada to disputes of this kind.

### Key holdings (verbatim from the judgment)

**Holding 1 — Presumption of resulting trust applies to gratuitous transfers from a parent to an adult child** (replaces the older presumption of advancement for adult children):

> "[I]t is common nowadays for ageing parents to transfer their assets into joint accounts with their adult children in order to have that child assist them in managing their financial affairs, there should be a rebuttable presumption that the adult child is holding the property in trust for the ageing parent to facilitate the free and efficient management of that parent's affairs."
> — Headnote, citing paras 33, 36, 40–41.

> "The presumption of resulting trust is a rebuttable presumption of law and general rule that applies to gratuitous transfers. When a transfer is challenged, the presumption allocates the legal burden of proof. Thus, where a transfer is made for no consideration, the onus is placed on the transferee to demonstrate that a gift was intended ... This is so because equity presumes bargains, not gifts."
> — Para 24.

> "[T]he presumption of advancement, which applies equally to fathers and mothers, is limited in its application to gratuitous transfers made by parents to minor children."
> — Headnote, citing paras 33, 36.

**Why it matters for Daughter:** Mary's joint accounts and joint investments are presumptively held in resulting trust for Mary's estate. The onus is on **Daughter** (the surviving joint holder / transferee) to prove Mary intended a gift of the right of survivorship. Not on Brother to prove the opposite.

**Holding 2 — Standard of proof to rebut: balance of probabilities (civil standard):**

> "The weight of recent authority, however, suggests that the civil standard, the balance of probabilities, is applicable to rebut the presumptions ... I see no reason to depart from the normal civil standard of proof. The evidence required to rebut both presumptions, therefore, is evidence of the transferor's contrary intention on the balance of probabilities."
> — Para 43.

**Why it matters for Daughter:** Daughter doesn't need to prove gift-intent beyond a reasonable doubt — just that it's more likely than not. But she still needs *evidence*, and right now (per `Background.md` Q2) we don't know whether any documented evidence of Mary's intent exists.

**Holding 3 — The right of survivorship vests inter vivos when the joint account is opened. What the parent "gifts" is the survivorship interest in whatever balance remains at death:**

> "[T]he rights of survivorship, both legal and equitable, vest when the joint account is opened and the gift of those rights is therefore *inter vivos* in nature."
> — Para 48.

> "The presumption of a resulting trust means in that context that it will fall to the surviving joint account holder to prove that the transferor intended to gift the right of survivorship to whatever assets are left in the account to the survivor."
> — Headnote, citing paras 48, 50, 53.

**Why it matters for Daughter:** The legal question isn't "did Mary mean to give Daughter the money?" — it's "did Mary, when she opened/added Daughter to each account or investment, mean to gift the *right of survivorship* (i.e., that whatever was left at Mary's death would go to Daughter alone)?" That is the precise intent that must be proved.

**Holding 4 — What evidence courts look at to determine intent:**

> "The evidence considered by a court may include the wording used in bank documents, the control and use of the funds in the account, the granting of a power of attorney, the tax treatment of the joint account, and evidence subsequent to the transfer if such evidence is relevant to the transferor's intention at the time of the transfer."
> — Headnote, citing paras 55, 59–62, 69.

The Court then discussed each evidentiary factor in turn:

- **Bank/brokerage documents** (paras 60–61): if the account-opening forms specifically address the beneficial interest or survivorship intent, "the clearer the evidence in the bank documents in question, the more weight that evidence should carry."
- **Control and use of funds during lifetime** (paras 62–63): "the transferor's retention of his or her exclusive beneficial interest in the account in his or her lifetime may support the finding of a resulting trust, unless other evidence proves that he or she intended to gift the right of survivorship to the transferee."
- **Power of attorney** (paras 67–68): a separately granted power of attorney can cut either way — courts will weigh it in context.
- **Tax treatment** (para 69): who declared/paid tax on the account's income matters, but is "not determinative" alone.

**Why it matters for Daughter:** This is the evidentiary checklist Daughter (and her estates lawyer) will need to work through.

On the facts confirmed so far, Mary's joint assets are likely to be treated the same way.

### Specific *Pecore* paragraph that maps to Mary's will

This is verbatim Mary's question. The Supreme Court framed the issue using almost identical language to Mary's situation: a will that leaves the residue to be split between two adult children, with joint accounts in the name of only one of them.

## Entry 2 — Companion case: Madsen Estate v. Saylor, 2007 SCC 18

**Captured:** 2026-05-14
**Source:** Supreme Court of Canada, decided same day as *Pecore* (2007-05-03).
**Citation:** *Madsen Estate v. Saylor*, 2007 SCC 18, [2007] 1 SCR 838.
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/2356/index.do (judgment body via `/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/2356/index.do?iframe=true`)
**Panel:** McLachlin C.J. and Bastarache, Binnie, LeBel, Deschamps, Fish, Abella, Charron and Rothstein JJ. (same 9 judges as *Pecore*).
**Decision:** Rothstein J. for the majority of 8; Abella J. dissenting (would have ordered a new trial). Appeal dismissed.
**Source quality:** Primary. Binding SCC authority.

### Why this case is the closer fit to Mary's situation

*Madsen* applied the *Pecore* framework on facts that are **a closer match to Mary's situation than *Pecore* itself** — and reached the **opposite conclusion from *Pecore***: the joint accounts and joint investments were held in resulting trust for the estate, not gifted to the surviving joint holder.

The *Madsen* facts (from paragraphs 5–10 of the judgment):

- Father added his adult daughter Patricia ("P") to his bank accounts in 1991 (a few years after his wife's death). The accounts had a right of survivorship designation. In 1997 the funds were moved into a new bank account and an investment account, also joint with right of survivorship.
- Father also gave Patricia a power of attorney.
- **Father retained sole control of the accounts during his lifetime and used the funds solely for his benefit.**
- **Father declared and paid all the income tax on the joint accounts.**
- The will: P and her two siblings to share half the estate.
- Joint accounts totalled approximately $185,000 at father's death.
- After father's death, Patricia (acting as executor) did not include the joint accounts in the estate distribution. Her siblings sued.

**Mapping to Mary's situation:**

| *Madsen* fact | Mary's situation |
|---|---|
| Father added adult daughter to existing accounts | ✓ same (Q1) |
| Right of survivorship designation in bank documents | pending institutional records (D-01a). Per *Madsen*, the designation alone is insufficient anyway. |
| Father retained sole control of accounts | ✓ same (Q3 — operated solely for Mary's benefit) |
| Father used funds solely for own benefit | ✓ same (Q3) |
| Father paid all the income tax on the accounts | likely ✓ — to be confirmed by tax accountant from prior-year T1s |
| Power of attorney also granted | **✓ same (D-04 confirmed 2026-05-14 — Mary granted Daughter a POA for Property)** |
| Will splits estate among children including daughter | ✓ same (Background.md) |
| Surviving daughter is also the executor | ✓ same (Background.md) |

The factual similarity is now overwhelming. **Every confirmable *Madsen* factor matches Mary's situation.** The one factor that *might* have shifted things (the right-of-survivorship designation in the bank papers) was itself insufficient on its own in *Madsen* and is in any case not yet retrieved from BMO.

**Additional contextual fact** (added 2026-05-14, revised same day for framing): the alternate executor named in Mary's will is Brother (also a residuary beneficiary). In *Madsen* the surviving daughter was sued by her siblings — but Mary's situation is cooperative, not adversarial. Daughter has expressed a settled intent to split the residue 50/50 with Brother per the will, regardless of how the *Pecore* question is characterized. The *Madsen*-tracking analysis above is therefore not predicting a Brother-vs-Daughter outcome — it is identifying which legal characterization fits Mary's facts. That characterization affects the mechanism of distribution, the tax filings, and the EAT exposure, but not the economic destination (which is the 50/50 split either way). See `findings/distribution-timing.md` Entry 6 for the cooperative-mechanism framing.

### Key holdings (verbatim from the judgment)

**Holding 1 — Presumption of resulting trust applied; daughter could not rebut:**

> "In the present case, a presumption of a resulting trust applies to the gratuitous transfer of assets by Patricia's father into the joint accounts with Patricia. The presumption of advancement has no application because Patricia was not a minor child of her father. Patricia therefore had the burden of rebutting the presumption of a resulting trust by showing that her father intended to gift the assets in the accounts to her on the balance of probabilities."
> — Para 17.

**Holding 2 — Bank documents specifying "right of survivorship" alone are NOT enough to rebut the presumption:**

This is the single most important holding of *Madsen* for Mary's purposes. The bank documents in *Madsen* did say "right of survivorship":

> "On the relevant financial institution documents, the father elected to have the joint accounts carry a right of survivorship. Patricia testified that both she and her father acknowledged that they understood at the time that this meant that on the death of one of the joint account holders, the other would become the sole owner."
> — Para 20.

And the actual TD Bank account agreement contained the words:

> "If the account *has a right of survivorship* then if any one or more of us dies any moneys standing to the credit of the account are to be subject to withdrawal by the survivor or, if more than one, by any one or more of the survivors..."
> — Para 25 (quoting the TD account agreement, emphasis in original).

Yet the Supreme Court found this insufficient to rebut the presumption:

> "Beyond the fact that both accounts were designated as carrying the right of survivorship, the banking documents do not contain any express reference to beneficial entitlement to the assets in the accounts."
> — Para 25.

> "[E]ven having regard to the financial institution documents and Patricia's testimony in relation to them, such evidence is insufficient to rebut the presumption of resulting trust. This conclusion is consistent with the trial judge's conclusion based on the evidence she did consider."
> — Para 29.

**Why it matters for Daughter:** A right-of-survivorship checkbox on the bank/brokerage account-opening form is — by itself — *not enough*. *Madsen* explicitly holds that a survivorship designation describes the legal mechanism by which title moves at death; it does not address the *beneficial* entitlement, which is the *Pecore* question. To rebut the presumption, Daughter would need documentary or testimonial evidence specifically directed at beneficial ownership / gift intent — not just the survivorship checkbox.

**Holding 3 — Control and tax-paying are not determinative but corroborate the presumption:**

> "As discussed in *Pecore*, at paras. 62‑66, the fact that a transferor maintains sole control over or use of funds in a joint account will not be determinative of whether a transferee is entitled to the balance in the account upon the transferor's death. Whether or not a transferor continues to pay tax on the income of the joint accounts is also not determinative."
> — Para 19.

In *Madsen*, the trial judge had found these factors persuasive in finding resulting trust; the Supreme Court reaffirmed they are evidence to consider but not determinative.

**Why it matters for Daughter:** Mary's exclusive use of the funds and (presumed) payment of all the income tax during her lifetime are corroborating factors. Not determinative on their own, but consistent with the presumption being upheld.

**Holding 4 — Practical remedy: daughter ordered to pay the joint-account value to the estate:**

> "Van Melle J. found that there was no evidence to support Brooks' position that her father intended to gift the contents of the joint accounts to her. As a result, she ordered that the joint bank account and joint investments be included in the estate and ordered Patricia to pay to the estate a sum of $185,000."
> — Para 13.

The Supreme Court upheld this remedy.

**Why it matters for Daughter:** *Madsen* establishes the remedy that applies in *contested* cases — payment of the joint-asset value back into the estate, for distribution per the will. Mary's situation is cooperative (Daughter intends to split the residue 50/50 with Brother per the will regardless), so this remedy is unlikely to be tested in court. But the underlying legal characterization still matters: it determines the cleanest mechanism for the distribution and the EAT/tax treatment. The lawyer's job is to advise on path selection (Path A — survivorship then gift; Path B — through the estate per the will) given the *Pecore*-tracking facts.

### Researcher's bottom line on Pecore + Madsen for Mary's facts

On every confirmed evidentiary factor, Mary's situation tracks *Madsen* rather than *Pecore*. The *one* factor that could meaningfully shift the analysis — Mary having made a clear written statement of gift-of-survivorship intent — has not been established yet (Q2 is "unsure, haven't checked"). Even a bank-document right-of-survivorship designation was insufficient on its own in *Madsen*. The structural similarity to *Madsen* — executor-daughter is the surviving joint holder; will splits estate among children; father retained control and paid the tax — is what makes this finding particularly weighty.

This does not mean the matter is "decided" — *Pecore*/Madsen is fact-sensitive and a lawyer reviewing all the original documents may find facts that change the picture. But the burden of proof is on Daughter, the present evidence does not appear to discharge that burden, and *Madsen* shows what the remedy looks like if it does not.

## Entry 4 — CRA position: deemed disposition at death and joint accounts

**Captured:** 2026-05-14
**Status:** Partially researched; primary CRA pages returned 403 to WebFetch in this session. Action item to retry via alternative route.

**What is established from search-result summaries (not yet verbatim-quoted from CRA):**

- Under *Income Tax Act* s. 70(5), a deceased person is **deemed to have disposed of all capital property immediately before death at fair market value**. This triggers a capital gain or loss on Mary's terminal T1 return.
- The deemed disposition applies regardless of how legal title moves on death. So even if the joint investments pass to Daughter by survivorship for *legal* purposes, the deemed-disposition rule still produces a taxable event on Mary's final return for any embedded capital gain accrued during her lifetime.
- A **rollover** to defer the capital gain is available only when the property passes to a **spouse or common-law partner** (or a qualifying spousal trust) under s. 70(6). There is **no equivalent rollover** for an adult child taking property by survivorship.
- The RIF ($805) is a separate regime. RIFs go to a named beneficiary outside the estate (Daughter is the sole beneficiary per `Background.md`). The full FMV of the RIF at death is generally included in income on Mary's terminal return (s. 146.3(6) ITA), with limited rollover possibilities not applicable here (Daughter is not Mary's spouse and the RIF is too small to be worth special handling).

**Why it matters for Daughter (Q1, Q3, Q5):**

1. The date 2026-04-21 is significant because that's the date of the **deemed disposition at fair market value**. Accrued capital gains on Mary's portion of the investments to that date are taxable on the terminal T1 return.
2. The "joint = no tax" view that lay people often hold is wrong for non-spouse joint holders. The deemed disposition runs even if the asset passes by survivorship.
3. If the *Pecore* analysis lands on resulting trust, Mary is treated as having owned 100% of the joint investments beneficially (not 50%), so the deemed disposition is on 100% of the embedded gain, not just half. If *Pecore* is rebutted and Mary is found to have gifted survivorship, the situation is more complex — there are CRA technical interpretations on this, to be captured in a future research pass.
