# Distribution Timing — When Can Daughter Disperse Funds to Beneficiaries?

**Topic:** Daughter's Question 2 — when can she safely distribute estate assets to herself and Brother? This file is about the *timing rules*, not the *amount* (that depends on the *Pecore* analysis — see `findings/jtwros-treatment.md`).

**Why this matters for Daughter (Q2, Q3, Q6):** Three different sets of rules govern this:

1. **Common-law executor's year** — a soft 12-month benchmark.
2. **Fiduciary duty under the will** — a hard rule: Daughter cannot distribute in a way that prejudices Brother.
3. **CRA clearance certificate framework (Income Tax Act s. 159(2))** — a hard rule: Daughter is personally liable for unpaid tax up to the value of what she distributes too early.

Distributing too early is the single largest *personal* financial risk Daughter faces in her capacity as executor.

## Entry 1 — The executor's year (common-law benchmark)

**Captured:** 2026-05-14
**Source:** Synthesis from multiple Ontario estates-practice secondary sources.
**Source quality:** Secondary (practice commentary). The concept itself is a long-established common-law principle; the secondary sources are useful for current practice, but for a definitive statement an Ontario estates lawyer should be consulted.

### The concept

Under the common law, executors are expected to wind up the estate — collect assets, pay debts and taxes, and distribute the residue — within roughly **12 months of the date of death**. This is the "executor's year."

It is a benchmark, not a deadline. Courts will not generally sanction an executor who reasonably takes longer (and many estates routinely do — especially where probate, tax filings, or disputes are pending). The doctrine protects executors from beneficiary pressure to distribute prematurely *and* protects beneficiaries from indefinite delay.

### What it means for Mary's situation

- Mary died 2026-04-21. The executor's year runs notionally to **2027-04-21**.
- Daughter is well within this window today (2026-05-14, less than four weeks after death). There is no pressure to distribute fast.
- The executor's year would *not* shield Daughter from the personal-liability risk under the *Income Tax Act* (Entry 3 below). The executor's year is a fairness-to-beneficiaries doctrine, not a tax-liability shield.

### Beneficiary rights during the executor's year

Brother, as a residuary beneficiary, has the right to:

- An accounting of estate dealings (informal during administration; a formal passing of accounts at the end if requested).
- Distribution within a reasonable time.
- Information about the assets, debts, and tax filings.

Brother does *not* have the right to demand distribution before debts and taxes are paid.

**Why it matters for Daughter:** The 12-month benchmark gives Daughter time to do this properly. There is no need to rush, and rushing creates exposure.

## Entry 2 — Executor's fiduciary duty to all beneficiaries

**Captured:** 2026-05-14
**Source:** Government of Ontario, "Administering estates" page.
**URL:** https://www.ontario.ca/page/administering-estates
**Date last updated:** 2024-03-05
**Source quality:** Primary (official Government of Ontario summary).

### The verbatim definition of an estate trustee's duties

> "Estate trustees are responsible for carrying out the wishes of the deceased person set out in the terms of the will (if there is one) and administering an estate in accordance with the law.
>
> ... they are responsible for:
> - winding up the affairs of the deceased
> - paying taxes, bills and any other debts
> - collecting the estate assets and distributing the residue of the estate to those who are entitled to it (for example, the remainder after all liabilities are paid)."

### How this intersects with the Pecore question

The executor's duties are owed to *all* beneficiaries — including Brother. If Daughter, in her capacity as executor, treats the joint accounts and investments as her own personal property (e.g., by signing the financial-advisor retitling papers to put them in her sole name), and *Pecore* later applies to put them back into the estate, Daughter will have:

1. Treated estate property as her own — a classic breach of fiduciary duty.
2. Done so in circumstances where the *Pecore* presumption was unrebutted on the facts she knew (resulting trust pattern: see `findings/jtwros-treatment.md`).
3. Acted contrary to Mary's testamentary intent (Mary's expressed wish in the will was a 50/50 split — see `Background.md`).

The remedy for breach of fiduciary duty by an executor includes returning the value to the estate (as in *Madsen Estate v. Saylor*, where Patricia was ordered to pay $185,000 back to the estate — see `findings/jtwros-treatment.md` Entry 2), removal as executor, and potentially costs.

**Why it matters for Daughter:** The retitling papers Daughter has already signed are the single most consequential action taken so far. They are reversible at the financial advisor's level (papers signed, retitling not yet completed), but become harder to reverse once the brokerage has executed the retitle. The fiduciary-duty exposure is *separate from and additional to* the CRA personal-liability exposure in Entry 3.

## Entry 3 — CRA clearance certificate and personal liability (the binding tax rule)

**Captured:** 2026-05-14
**Source:** Government of Canada — Canada Revenue Agency.

### Primary sources

- [Apply for a clearance certificate — CRA](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/individuals/life-events/doing-taxes-someone-died/clearance-certificate.html) (page referenced — could not be fetched directly in this research session, content drawn from CRA-sourced search summaries)
- [Form TX19, Asking for a Clearance Certificate — CRA](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/forms/tx19.html)
- [Information Circular IC82-6R13, Clearance Certificate — CRA](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/publications/ic82-6/clearance-certificate.html) (current version)

**Source quality:** Primary (CRA's own published policy). Statutory grounding: subsection 159(2) of the *Income Tax Act*.

### The rule (from CRA's published guidance, via search-summary extract — to be re-verified verbatim in next pass when direct fetch is possible)

> "Subsection 159(2) of the Income Tax Act requires a legal representative to obtain a clearance certificate before distributing property that he or she controls in their capacity as the legal representative. As a legal representative, if you distribute the property without a certificate, you are liable for any unpaid amounts."

> "[I]f the legal representative does not get a clearance certificate before they distribute assets and there are any tax amounts owing under the Income Tax Act or Part IX of the Excise Tax Act to the CRA, they are personally liable for unpaid amounts, up to the value of the amount of assets distributed."

> "Once the legal representative has received a clearance certificate, they are then able to distribute assets without the risk of being personally liable for unpaid amounts ... The liability then rests on the estate, trust, corporation, beneficiaries, or other people who have received the distributed assets."

> "You do not need a clearance certificate before each distribution, as long as you keep enough property to pay any liability to us."

### What this means in plain language

1. Daughter, as legal representative, is **personally on the hook** for any unpaid CRA tax owed by Mary or by Mary's estate — up to the value of whatever she distributes — if she distributes before a clearance certificate is issued.
2. To get a clearance certificate, Daughter files Form TX19 *after* she has:
   - Filed Mary's terminal T1 return (and obtained the Notice of Assessment).
   - Filed any required T3 estate-trust return(s) for income earned by the estate after death (and obtained those Notices of Assessment).
   - Paid all taxes shown owing on those assessments.
3. CRA then reviews and, if satisfied, issues the clearance certificate (TX19 processing has historically taken several months — see CRA's service standards page; current target is roughly 120 days under normal circumstances, but actual times vary).
4. **Once the clearance certificate is issued, Daughter's personal exposure ends** for matters covered up to that date. Liability for any subsequently discovered tax shifts to the estate / beneficiaries.

### The "safe interim distribution" exception

CRA's guidance recognizes that executors don't need a clearance certificate before *every* distribution, as long as they hold back enough to cover any potential CRA liability. In Mary's case this matters because:

- A modest interim distribution to Brother (e.g., from cash that is clearly *not* the subject of the *Pecore* dispute and once any tax owing is provisioned for) may be possible before clearance.
- But Daughter would need to be confident that what is held back is sufficient. Given the *Pecore* uncertainty — Mary's terminal-T1 tax depends on whether the joint assets are 100% Mary's beneficially (resulting trust) or were transferred to Daughter as a gift of survivorship — the tax exposure is itself uncertain. Holding back conservatively is the safe play.

## Entry 4 — Timeline for Mary's estate

**Captured:** 2026-05-14

Combining the rules above, here is a non-binding timeline for Mary's estate. Dates are calculated from death (2026-04-21) and are projections, not deadlines except where noted as statutory.

| Period | Event | Statutory? |
|---|---|---|
| 0 – ~1 month | Notify bank, find will, secure assets, get death certificate(s), begin to identify assets | No |
| ~1 – 3 months | Resolve the *Pecore* question with an Ontario estates lawyer; pause / undo retitling if necessary; decide whether probate is needed (bank's call) | No |
| ~3 – 6 months | If probate is required, prepare and file the application with the Superior Court of Justice; pay EAT deposit | No (but EAT is due when the application is filed) |
| By **2027-04-30** | **File Mary's 2026 terminal T1 return** — due April 30 of the year following death under *Income Tax Act* s. 150(1)(d) (the special "6 months after death" rule in s. 150(1)(b) does *not* apply because Mary died before November). Mary's 2025 T1 was filed before her death, so no prior-year filing deadline remains. | **Yes** |
| 180 days after Certificate of Appointment issued (if probate applied for) | **File the Estate Information Return** with Ontario Ministry of Finance | **Yes** |
| After Mary's T1 is assessed | File Form TX19 (Request for Clearance Certificate) | No, but advisable |
| ~3 – 6 months after TX19 | Receive clearance certificate | No (CRA service standard) |
| After clearance | **Safe full distribution to Daughter and Brother** | — |

### When can Daughter realistically distribute?

- **Earliest fully-safe distribution:** when the clearance certificate is in hand. Realistically, **late 2027 to mid-2028** depending on filing timing and CRA processing.
- **Earliest safe interim distribution:** once the *Pecore* question is resolved with the lawyer's involvement, debts are paid, terminal T1 estimated tax is provisioned for, and an explicit holdback is maintained. Could be sometime in **late 2026 or 2027**.
- **What Daughter should not do now (2026-05-14):** distribute anything to herself (including completing the retitling), distribute anything to Brother, sign anything that the financial advisor characterizes as "this is yours, take it" without first speaking to an estates lawyer.

## Entry 6 — The alternate-executor fact and Daughter's settled distribution intent

**Captured:** 2026-05-14 (revised 2026-05-14 — re-framed around cooperative intent after user feedback).
**Source:** `Background.md` Q&A captured 2026-05-14 (second pass, D-06, D-08, and the settled-intent paragraph).

### The two facts that frame this entry

1. **Alternate executor:** Mary's will names **Brother** as the alternate (backup) executor. Brother is also a residuary beneficiary entitled to an equal share of the estate.
2. **Daughter's settled distribution intent:** Daughter intends to split the remaining estate assets 50/50 with Brother after expenses are paid, regardless of how the *Pecore* legal question is characterized. This is a settled position, not contingent on legal compulsion.

These two facts together mean the analysis below is about **mechanism selection and procedural coherence**, not about a contested distribution.

### Why these facts still matter (cooperative framing)

Even though the siblings are aligned on the equal-split outcome, the alternate-executor fact and the *Pecore* analysis affect how Daughter administers the estate cleanly:

1. **Renunciation as a process simplification is unattractive.** If Daughter were to renounce in favour of the alternate, Brother would assume the executor role and administer the estate himself. There is no estates-law obstacle to this if both siblings are aligned — it is just unnecessary friction. Daughter, as the surviving joint holder and the person closest to Mary's affairs, is the natural administrator. Brother as alternate is a useful backup if something happens to Daughter; he is not a path that helps the *Pecore* question.

2. **Communications with Brother are easier when the lawyer has structured the choice.** Because Daughter and Brother both have a legitimate interest in the *mechanism* used (Path A or Path B — see below and `Background.md`), it is cleaner to present a coherent picture once than to discuss in fragments while the analysis is still in progress. Daughter's choice to wait for the lawyer (D-08) reflects this — it is a matter of clarity, not of guarding against an adversarial sibling.

3. **The mechanism choice itself has both options on the table.** Daughter's cooperative intent means either path can be chosen on its merits. Neither path is excluded by a need to win an argument with Brother:
   - **Path A — survivorship then gift:** Daughter completes the retitling, takes the joint assets in her sole name by survivorship, then gives Brother roughly half as a personal gift. Avoids EAT on the joint assets (~$6,450 saved). But the brokerage records and tax filings would reflect a different reality than what is actually happening, which can create CRA scrutiny and documentation friction.
   - **Path B — through the estate per the will:** Joint assets are recognized as Mary's beneficially (the *Pecore*-consistent characterization on Mary's facts), pass through probate if BMO requires it, and are distributed per the will. EAT of ~$6,450 may apply. Filings, accounting, and distribution are coherent and defensible.

4. **The lawyer can help document the chosen path so it is defensible later.** This is true under either path. Path B is generally easier to document because it aligns with the legal characterization that Mary's facts support; Path A requires careful structuring to avoid CRA queries about the inconsistency between "Daughter owns it" (legal records) and "split equally with Brother" (actual disposition).

### What Brother's alternate-executor role does mean, in practice

- A clean backup if Daughter cannot continue as executor for any reason (e.g., illness). Routine and helpful.
- A reason to keep Brother informed of administrative steps as a courtesy (he has standing as both alternate executor and residuary beneficiary).
- Not a structural threat in this case. Daughter and Brother are aligned on outcome; the lawyer's role is to help them choose a clean mechanism, not to manage a dispute.

### Action items arising

- **L-01, L-03, L-05** in `questions-open.md`: the lawyer's role in mechanism selection and in framing the explanation for Brother is the centre of the engagement, but the framing is collaborative, not adversarial.
- **L-07** (joint vs separate briefing format with Brother) is less urgent given cooperative intent — either format works; the practical question is what Daughter and Brother find most comfortable. The lawyer's role is to ensure both siblings hear the same explanation of the mechanism choice.
- **D-12** (potential neutral co-executor candidate) is largely moot under cooperative intent. Retained as a contingency only.

## Entry 7 — How this answers Daughter's Q2, Q3, Q6

**Q2 — When can Daughter disperse funds to beneficiaries?**

The earliest fully-safe distribution is after the CRA clearance certificate is issued. That depends on filing Mary's terminal T1 (due 2027-04-30), having it assessed, paying any tax owing, filing Form TX19, and CRA processing the request (~4 months under current service standards). Realistically, late 2027 to mid-2028 for full distribution. Interim partial distributions are possible earlier with adequate holdbacks, but only after the *Pecore* allocation is settled.

**Q3 — When will taxes be due?**

Two key tax dates:

- **Mary's terminal T1:** due 2027-04-30 (the later of April 30 of the year following death and 6 months after death).
- **Ontario EAT:** payable as a deposit when the probate application is filed, if probate is applied for. Timing depends on the bank's decision and Daughter's lawyer's advice.
- **T3 estate trust returns:** if the estate earns any income after the date of death (e.g., interest on bank balances while the estate is being administered), a T3 estate return is required for the estate's tax year. The first T3 may be due as early as 90 days after the chosen year-end. (More detail to be added in a future research pass.)

**Q6 — Rough sequence of events:**

See Entry 4 timeline above. The single most important sequencing point is that the *Pecore* analysis (with an Ontario estates lawyer) needs to come *before* any distribution — including the financial-advisor retitling.
